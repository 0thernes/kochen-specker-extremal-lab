Certified exact minima of Kochen-Specker sets inside coordinate pools: 31 and 33 rays in dimension three, 18 in dimension four, 29 in dimension five

Alexander S. Donahue — 0thernes LLC, Washington, USA — 0_0@0thernes.art — ORCID 0009-0006-8168-9648

Version 1.0.0, 10 November 2026. Research preprint, not peer reviewed.

What this paper does and does not do. The smallest Kochen-Specker (KS) set in dimension three is an open problem: it has between 24 and 31 rays. This paper does not solve that problem and does not contain a KS set smaller than a published one. It answers a restricted question exactly: inside a given finite pool of rays, what is the smallest KS set? For the pools of Table 2 the answer equals the size of the smallest published set of that kind (31, 33, 18 or 29 rays), and the answers come with certificates that can be re-checked without trusting the search programs; Table 2 says which kind for each pool. Several of the values were reported before without proof: 31 for integer coordinates up to 2, 3 and 4, 33 for sixth roots of unity, and 29 as the smallest set generated from coordinates 0, ±1 in dimension five (Section 1.3). New here, to our knowledge: the proofs with their certificates, including Lean 4 kernel proofs for two pools; the exact minimum 33 for twelfth, eighteenth and twenty-fourth roots of unity; and the complete lists of minimum sets for integer coordinates up to 3 and 4 and for sixth and twelfth roots of unity.

How it was made. The programs, proofs and text were produced by an AI system (Claude, Anthropic) at the request and under the direction of the author, who is responsible for the release (Section 9).

Abstract

A Kochen-Specker set is a finite set of rays admitting no assignment of 0 and 1 that gives exactly one 1 to every orthogonal basis and never 1 to two orthogonal rays. We determine the minimum size of such a set inside prescribed finite pools of rays. Every KS set must contain a basis missed by any set of pairwise non-orthogonal rays; this turns the question into a covering problem whose constraints are individually trivial to verify. Two necessary conditions for critical KS sets, a symmetry normal form, and a case analysis make it tractable in pools of up to 651 rays. Three independent exact methods are used and each result carries a certificate. Results: (1) among rays of dimension three with integer coordinates of absolute value at most 2, 3 or 4, the minimum is 31 and the minimum sets are exactly the six images of one Conway-Kochen-type set under the symmetries of the cube; (2) among rays with coordinates 0 or N-th roots of unity, N = 6, 12, 18, 24, the minimum is 33, unique up to symmetry for N = 6 and 12; (3) among the 40 rays of dimension four with coordinates 0, ±1 the minimum is 18, attained only by the 16 known sets inside the 24 rays of Peres; (4) among the 121 rays of dimension five with coordinates 0, ±1 the minimum is 29, the size of the set of Cabello, Estebaranz and García-Alcaine. The values in (1), the value for N = 6 in (2), and 29 as the smallest set generated from the coordinates of (4) had been reported without proof. The minimum values in (1) for bound 2 and in (3) are proved in Lean 4 without Mathlib and checked by its kernel. The remaining statements rest on unsatisfiability proofs checked by a separate proof checker, with the exceptions listed in Section 4. Randomised searches in further pools in dimensions three to eight find nothing smaller than the published sets. The general minimal-KS problem is not settled by any of this.

1. Introduction

1.1 The Kochen-Specker theorem and its minimal sets

Kochen and Specker [1] and Bell [2] showed that quantum theory is incompatible with models that assign every projective measurement a pre-existing outcome independent of the other compatible measurements performed with it. The combinatorial core is a finite set of rays in a Hilbert space of dimension d ≥ 3 that cannot be 0/1-colored: no assignment gives exactly one 1 to each orthogonal basis while never giving 1 to two orthogonal rays. Such a set is a Kochen-Specker set. The original set had 117 rays in dimension three; smaller sets followed [3, 4, 5], and the smallest KS set in each dimension became a problem in its own right. KS sets are also the resource behind state-independent contextuality [26] and bipartite perfect quantum strategies [18, 35].

1.2 State of knowledge (meta-analysis)

Table 1 collects the smallest known KS set and the best proven lower bound in each dimension, as far as we could establish them from the primary sources on 9 and 10 November 2026. Counts follow the standard definition of Section 2: only the rays of the set are counted. Papers that use hypergraphs with every basis completed report larger numbers for the same sets in dimension three (the 31-ray set is "51-37").

Only dimension four is settled. In dimension three the lower bound rose from 18 [8] to 22 [9] to 24 [10, 11] by exhaustive enumeration of candidate orthogonality graphs, with the final unembeddability step done by an SMT solver; the authors of [11] estimate more than a hundred CPU years for the next order. In dimensions five and above the only proven bound is the dimension-independent 18 of [12], and Pavičić and Waegell [32] write only that it "seems likely" that the smallest sets in dimensions four to eight are known.

Restricted results exist in dimension three. Trandafir and Cabello [18] proved that the Conway-Kochen set is rigid and that no KS set with 30 or fewer rays arises from the 13-ray set of Yu and Oh [26] by their completion procedure, and conjectured that 31 is the minimum. Li, Bright, Trandafir, Cabello and Ganesh [19] enumerated, with proof certificates, all KS sets with up to 33 rays containing the 25-ray completion of the Yu-Oh set. Pavičić and Megill [16, 17] and Pavičić [31] generate very large classes of critical sets from all vectors with components in a small set (their "master" sets) and report the smallest members they obtain; these are computational reports, without a proof that the generation is exhaustive and without certificates. Integer vectors can carry KS sets only because their directions are not rational points of the sphere, which are colorable [24, 25]; see also [23].

1.3 The pool problem, prior work on it, and what is new here

We fix a finite pool of rays and ask for the exact minimum size of a KS set inside it. A pool result says nothing about rays outside the pool, but the question is exactly solvable, gives matching upper and lower bounds, and tests the conjectured optimality of the records in the places where they live.

Prior work on this question, as far as we found it:

  • Kernaghan [22], a preprint whose computations and text were, by its own statement, generated by a language model, surveys coordinate alphabets in dimension three. It reports as exact, by a SAT procedure for which no certificate is given, the minimum 31 for the 49 rays with coordinates in {0, ±1, ±2} and the minimum 33 for the 57-ray pool of sixth roots of unity, and it lists 31 also for coordinates up to 3. By randomised minimisation it finds six distinct 31-ray sets in the 49-ray pool (one remark there speaks instead of 162 distinct sets, six up to automorphism). It proves that the pool of N-th roots of unity contains a KS set if and only if 6 divides N. For N = 12, 18, 24 it gives only upper bounds, and it has no dimension above three.
  • A note on the forum ProblemHive [33], posted in October 2026 by an AI agent, reports the minimum 31 for integer coordinates up to 2, 3 and 4, obtained with CP-SAT and the same kind of coloring cut as in Section 3.1. It states itself that its solver logs are not a certificate.
  • In dimension four the bound 18 holds for all KS sets [7], and in every dimension [12]. The 18-ray sets inside the 24 rays of Peres are known: there are 16 of them [39].
  • In dimension five, Pavičić and Megill [16] generate from the components 0, ±1 a master set with 105 rays and 136 bases, which is the core of our pool B(5, 1), and report that the smallest critical set of its class has 29 rays and 16 bases, with two non-isomorphic sets of that size (also [32]), one of which had been obtained in [15]. No proof that nothing smaller exists is given. Their coloring rule constrains only rays that share a basis of the set, so every KS set in their sense is one in the sense of Section 2, and a lower bound proved here applies to their sets as well.
  • Formal proofs exist for other statements: non-colorability of Peres's 33 rays in HOL Light [36], the KS paradox in Lean [37], case-tree certificates of non-embeddability replayed in Lean for the geometric half of the bound 24 [34], and a contextuality degree in Lean [38]. We found no formal proof of a minimality statement for KS sets, and none for the Conway-Kochen or the 18-ray set.

Against this background we claim as new, to our knowledge and within the search described in Section 7:

  1. proofs with certificates for the results: Lean 4 kernel proofs for the 49-ray pool and for the 40-ray pool of dimension four, replayable search trees, and unsatisfiability proofs checked by a separate checker;
  2. complete lists of the minimum sets: exactly six for integer coordinates up to 3 and up to 4 (the same six as for 2), exactly 3 and 12 for sixth and twelfth roots of unity, each list a single orbit under the symmetries of the pool; and the fact that the 40-ray pool of dimension four contains no 18-ray KS set besides the 16 known ones [39];
  3. the exact minimum 33 for twelfth, eighteenth and twenty-fourth roots of unity;
  4. the proof that 29 is the minimum for coordinates 0, ±1 in dimension five, where 29 had been reported as the smallest set obtained [16], and the fact that every KS set in that pool contains at least three coordinate axes;
  5. the method beyond the coloring cut itself: necessary conditions for critical sets as constraints, a symmetry normal form, an encoding whose unsatisfiability proofs are checked independently, and tree certificates with a checker proved sound in Lean (the pattern of certificate and verified checker used in [34], applied here to a minimality statement).

We do not claim the values 31, 33, 18, 29, which are the sizes of published sets [3, 20, 5, 6], nor the coloring-cut idea, which also appears in [33] and, in the form of an unsatisfiable-subset loop, in [22].

2. Definitions

A pool is a finite set V of rays (one-dimensional subspaces) of a d-dimensional real or complex inner-product space, d ≥ 3. Two rays are orthogonal if their inner product vanishes. A basis is a set of d mutually orthogonal rays of V.

A subset S of V is colorable if there is f : S → {0, 1} such that

  1. f(u) + f(v) ≤ 1 whenever u, v ∈ S are orthogonal, and
  2. every basis contained in S has a ray with f = 1.

By rule 1 a basis inside S then has exactly one ray of value 1. If no such f exists, S is a Kochen-Specker set. Only the rays of S count; orthogonal pairs constrain f whether or not they extend to a basis inside S. Subsets of colorable sets are colorable. A KS set is critical if all its proper subsets are colorable; every KS set contains a critical one, so the minimum is attained by a critical set.

Two families of pools are used. B(d, H) is the set of rays spanned by non-zero integer vectors of \(\mathbb{Z}^d\) with all coordinates of absolute value at most H. U(d, N) is the set of rays spanned by non-zero vectors of \(\mathbb{C}^d\) with entries in \(\{0\} \cup \mu_N\), where \(\mu_N\) is the group of N-th roots of unity. Orthogonality is decided exactly: by integer dot products in B(d, H), and in U(d, N) by reducing the inner product, an integer combination of N-th roots of unity, modulo the N-th cyclotomic polynomial.

3. Method

Complete proofs of the statements of this section are in paper/PROOFS.md in the repository; they are short and are outlined here.

3.1 Coloring cuts

Call I ⊆ V independent if no two of its rays are orthogonal, and let Miss(I) be the set of bases of V containing no ray of I.

Lemma 1. S ⊆ V is a KS set if and only if, for every independent I ⊆ V, S contains a basis of Miss(I).

If S contains no basis of Miss(I), giving 1 to the rays of I inside S colors S. Conversely the rays of value 1 of a coloring form an independent set I with no basis of Miss(I) inside S.

So every independent set I yields a cut, the clause "S contains at least one basis of Miss(I)", valid for every KS set and certified by I alone: one checks that I is independent and recomputes Miss(I). Symmetries of the pool map cuts to cuts. In particular, if C ⊆ V is colorable, every KS set meets the complement of C.

3.2 Necessary conditions for critical sets

Lemma 3. If T is a critical KS set and v ∈ T, then (C1) v lies in a basis contained in T, and (C2) v is orthogonal to at least d rays of T.

For (C1), a coloring of T without v extends by f(v) = 0 if no basis through v lies in T. For (C2), if v had at most d − 1 partners they would be the rest of the unique basis b through v; then f(v) = 1 if the rest of b is all 0, and f(v) = 0 otherwise, extends the coloring.

Applied to the pool itself, the lemma allows the repeated deletion of rays that lie in no basis or have fewer than d partners; what remains is the core. Where the "master" hypergraphs of [16, 17] exist for the same vectors, our cores have their sizes: 105 rays and 136 bases for B(5, 1), 1185 and 3596 for B(5, 2), 332 and 1408 for B(6, 1), 805 and 9936 for B(7, 1), 834 and 1609 for U(6, 3). This is an external check of the pool construction.

3.3 Counterexample-guided search

Take Boolean variables \(x_v\) (ray chosen) and \(y_b\) (basis chosen), with \(y_b\) true exactly when all rays of b are chosen. For a bound k the candidate problem asks for an assignment with at most k rays chosen that satisfies (C1), (C2) and all cuts collected so far. A solution S is tested: if it is not colorable, a KS set with at most k rays has been found. Otherwise a coloring of S is extended to an independent set of the whole pool that misses as few bases as possible; its cut is violated by S and is added, together with its images under the symmetries of the pool. If the candidate problem becomes infeasible, the pool contains no KS set with at most k rays, by Lemmas 1 and 3. In a controlled run on B(3, 4) the conditions (C1) and (C2) reduce the number of rounds from 15 to 6 and the number of cuts from 307 to 123, with no gain in running time (results/ablate_c1c2_box3_4_k30.json).

3.4 Search trees

For small pools we also use a plain exhaustive search whose trace is a certificate. A node holds an independent set I and one child for each basis of Miss(I) that can still be added within the budget of k rays without completing a basis already excluded on the way; the subtree of a child treats the sets that contain its basis and none of the bases of the earlier siblings. A node without children is closed because I colors every remaining admissible set. Lemma 5 of paper/PROOFS.md states the checking rule and proves by induction that an accepted tree shows every set of at most k rays to be colorable. The rule uses neither (C1) nor (C2). The dual certificate, that one given set is not colorable, is a case tree on which ray of a basis has value 1.

3.5 Symmetry and cases

In B(d, H) the signed coordinate permutations are symmetries. Every set can be brought to a normal form in which the coordinates are sorted by the number of chosen rays with a non-zero entry there and, for \(j = 1, \dots, d-1\), the ray \(e_0 - e_j\) is chosen only if \(e_0 + e_j\) is (Lemma 6). For B(5, 1) we also use:

Lemma 7. Every basis of B(5, 1) contains a coordinate axis, and every KS set in B(5, 1) contains at least three coordinate axes.

The second part holds because the pool with any three axes removed is colorable (an explicit coloring is in results/lemma7_d5_axes.json). A critical KS set therefore has, up to a coordinate permutation, exactly the axes \(\{e_0, e_1, e_2\}\), or \(\{e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3\}\), or all five, and the three cases are searched separately. The last case is split further into 81 sub-cases ("cubes") by the states of the four pairs \((e_0 + e_j,\ e_0 - e_j)\): neither ray chosen, only the first, or both.

3.6 Three engines and what has to be trusted

  • Engine A solves the candidate problem with the CP-SAT solver of OR-Tools [29].
  • Engine B encodes the candidate problem from scratch in conjunctive normal form, re-verifying each cut from its independent set and using two-sided sequential counters for the counting constraints, and solves it with the CDCL solver Glucose [28] through PySAT [27], which emits a DRUP proof [30]. The proof is checked by verify/drup_check.c, a forward reverse-unit-propagation checker of about 250 lines written for this project and tested on correct proofs, corrupted proofs and satisfiable formulas. Where the register says so, the same formula was also given to a second solver, CaDiCaL [40], and its proof was checked by the same checker.
  • Engine C is the exhaustive tree search of Section 3.4. Its certificates are replayed by an independent JavaScript program (verify/verify_cert.mjs, written from the specification alone, which also recomputes orthogonality and all bases from the integer vectors) and, for two pools, by a checker whose soundness is proved in Lean 4.

For a result marked "proof-checked" the trusted parts are the pool construction, the encoder, the verification of the cuts, the proof checker, and Lemmas 1, 3, 6, 7; the solvers are not trusted. For a result marked "Lean" the trusted parts are the Lean kernel and the reading of the theorem statement and of the few definitions it uses (Section 5); tables, certificates and checkers are not trusted.

4.1 Dimension three, integer coordinates

Theorem 1. Let H ∈ {2, 3, 4}. Every KS set contained in B(3, H) has at least 31 rays. There are exactly six KS sets with 31 rays in B(3, H); they have coordinates of absolute value at most 2 and are the images of one of them under signed coordinate permutations.

The 31-ray sets have 71 orthogonal pairs and 17 bases. The Conway-Kochen set consists of vectors with coordinates in {0, ±1, ±2}, so by the theorem it is one of the six; the count of six agrees with the six equivalent versions of that set noted in [18] and with the six sets found by randomised minimisation in [22]. For H = 2 the statement "minimum 31" is the Lean theorem KS.Box3H2.minimum_ks_size_vectors. The minimum 31 for these three pools was reported without certificates in [22] (H = 2 as exact, H = 3 in a table) and in [33] (H = 2, 3, 4); the proofs, and the statement that for H = 3 and 4 there are no minimum sets besides the six, are new. Enlarging the pool from 49 to 289 rays adds 156 rays to the core and no new minimum set. The best coloring of the whole pool misses 1 of 26 bases for H = 2, 2 of 50 for H = 3, 7 of 110 for H = 4 and 14 of 266 for H = 5. For H = 5 (577 rays, core 421) the exact search was stopped without an answer after more than an hour, and we make no claim.

4.2 Dimension three, roots of unity

Theorem 2. For N ∈ {6, 12, 18, 24} every KS set contained in U(3, N) has at least 33 rays, and 33 is attained. In U(3, 6) there are exactly 3 KS sets with 33 rays and in U(3, 12) exactly 12; in both pools they form a single orbit under coordinate permutations, multiplication of single coordinates by N-th roots of unity and complex conjugation.

The 33-ray sets have 78 orthogonal pairs and 14 bases, the parameters of the complex set of [20, 21]. The value for N = 6 was reported in [22]; for N = 12, 18, 24 only upper bounds were known. By the theorem of [22], U(3, N) contains a KS set exactly when 6 divides N; our computations agree for N ≤ 12 (for N = 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 the core is empty). Whether the minimum is 33 for every multiple of 6 is open.

4.3 Dimension four

Theorem 3. Every KS set contained in B(4, 1) has at least 18 rays; there are exactly 16 KS sets with 18 rays in B(4, 1), forming one orbit under signed coordinate permutations.

The bound 18 holds for all KS sets in dimension four [7] and in every dimension [12]. The 16 sets are the 16 known 18-ray sets inside the 24 rays of Peres [39]: none of them uses a ray of the pool with three non-zero coordinates (results/peres24_check.json). What the enumeration adds is that these 16 further rays produce no other 18-ray KS set. The theorem is included because the Lean proof of its first statement, KS.Box4H1.minimum_ks_size_vectors, is to our knowledge the first proof of a minimality statement for KS sets checked by a proof assistant. The count of 16 sets is not part of the Lean theorem; it rests on the enumeration and its checked proof (Table 2).

Engine A closed only the three-axis case. All three cases were closed by engine B with Glucose and, on the same formulas, by a second SAT solver, CaDiCaL [40]; the proof checks are recorded in Table 3 and in the register.

4.5 Searches for smaller sets

A randomised walk on critical KS sets (remove a few rays, then repair to a critical KS set that keeps as much of the old set as possible) was run for 10 to 15 minutes in each pool of Table 4. It never found a set smaller than the smallest published one, and in every pool but one it reached that size, usually within seconds. The exception is U(3, 24), where the walk stopped at 36 rays although the pool contains 33-ray sets; this shows how weak a negative result of such a search is.

5. Formal verification in Lean 4

The Lean development (lean/, Lean 4.35.0, no Mathlib) has four layers.

  1. KS/Basic.lean defines pools as tables of bit masks, colorings, and the two certificate checkers check (Section 3.4) and refute, and proves their soundness: KS.small_sets_colorable (an accepted tree certificate implies that every set of at most k rays is colorable) and KS.not_colorable_of_refute (an accepted refutation implies that the given set has no coloring). While these were being proved, the first versions of both checkers turned out to accept malformed certificates; the definitions were corrected, and KS/Tests.lean records that the counterexamples are now rejected.
  2. KS/Spec.lean states the problem without tables. boxRays d H is the list of integer vectors of length d with entries between −H and H whose first non-zero entry is positive and whose entries have greatest common divisor 1: one representative of every ray of B(d, H). VColorable rays d T says that the set T of positions in the list rays has a coloring, where orthogonal means "dot product zero" and a basis is an increasing list of d positions of pairwise orthogonal vectors. Two theorems show that the table notion and this vector notion of colorability agree whenever a table passes three checks: its orthogonality entries are right, every listed basis consists of d mutually orthogonal rays, and every such set is listed.
  3. Each data module lists the vectors, the orthogonality table and the bases, proves the three checks by kernel evaluation, and proves that its list of vectors is boxRays d H and that every non-zero integer vector of the box is parallel to exactly one of them.
  4. The certificates are evaluated by the kernel (decide +kernel). Together with the layers above this gives
theorem KS.Box3H2.minimum_ks_size_vectors :
    (∃ T, T < 2 ^ 49 ∧ card 49 T = 31 ∧ ¬ VColorable (boxRays 3 2) 3 T) ∧
    (∀ T, T < 2 ^ 49 → card 49 T ≤ 30 → VColorable (boxRays 3 2) 3 T)

and the analogous KS.Box4H1.minimum_ks_size_vectors with boxRays 4 1, 40, 18 and 17. A set of rays is a natural number T read as a bit mask of positions in the list, and card counts its elements. The statement mentions no table and no certificate: to trust it one reads the definitions of boxRays, dot, card, Sub and VColorable, about 25 lines of Lean. The axioms used are propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound; there is no sorry and no native_decide. Building everything from scratch takes about one minute. The Lean theorems cover the two minimum values only; the counts of minimum sets and the other pools rest on the evidence of Table 2.

6. An operational check on the Tsotchke stack

The 31-ray set was also exercised on four pieces of the Tsotchke software stack, all classical programs: the Moonlab state-vector simulator (WebAssembly build), the Fubini-Study kernel of the quantum geometric tensor library, the integer mixers of quantum_rng as a reproducible pseudo-random stream, and an exact-integer program in the Eshkol language. A qutrit is embedded in two simulated qubits. The 71 orthogonal pairs and 17 bases are recovered from simulated overlaps and from Fubini-Study distances equal to π/2. For 2000 pseudo-random states the 17 basis sums of outcome probabilities equal 1 to within \(2 \times 10^{-15}\), so the sum over bases is 17 for every state, whereas exhaustive search shows that an assignment obeying rule 1 gives a 1 to at most 16 of the 17 bases. This restates the state-independent gap of the set in operational terms. It is a simulation, not an experiment, and no quantum randomness is involved.

7. Limits, prior art, open questions

  • Scope. A pool result excludes smaller KS sets only inside the pool. The general problem in dimension three (24 to 31) and in dimensions five and above (18 to the records of Table 1) is open.
  • Prior art and the word "new". Literature was searched on 9 and 10 November 2026 on arXiv, Zenodo, GitHub and the open web, by topic and by the distinctive numbers. What we found is in Section 1.3. Reference [33] is a forum post that could be read but not independently dated beyond its own status line. The first search missed two published facts: the report of 29 as the smallest set for the coordinates 0, ±1 in dimension five [16], and the 16 sets inside the 24 rays of Peres [39]. Both were found in a review of the manuscript before release, and the claims of Section 1.3 were narrowed accordingly. Preprints in this area appear weekly, several of them produced with AI systems, and a further overlap we did not find is possible.
  • What is certified to which level. Table 2 and the register say which rows have a proof check and which rest on solver answers. The randomised searches prove nothing about optimality.
  • Open questions. Is 31 the minimum in B(3, H) for all H? Is 33 the minimum in U(3, N) for every multiple N of 6? Is 21 the minimum in U(6, 3), and 34 in the E8 pool? Is 29 the minimum in dimension five without restriction on the coordinates? Does B(5, 1) contain a KS set with 29 or 30 rays and all five coordinate axes?
